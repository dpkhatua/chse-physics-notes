Electrostatics

Two kinds of charge, one law of force, and every trick for adding them up.

1 What Is Electrostatics?

Electrostatics is the branch of physics dealing with the properties of electricity at rest — i.e. stationary (static) charge. Stationary charge is produced on insulating substances like amber, ebonite, or glass by rubbing them with fur or silk. The key properties studied under electrostatics are electric charge, electric field, electric potential, dielectric polarisation, and capacitance.

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter, carried by particles like electrons and protons, alongside their mass. A body can gain or lose electrons — a body with an excess of electrons or a deficit compared to its neutral state is called a charged body. Charge is best understood not by what it is, but by what it does — it produces forces on other charges.

2 Characteristics of Electric Charge

  1. Two kinds of charge: the electric force between two electrons at a distance apart is repulsive, same as between two protons. But between a proton and an electron at the same separation, the force is attractive. Hence there must be two distinct kinds of charge — called positive and negative.
  2. Charge is quantised: the smallest unit of charge (the basic/elementary charge) equals the magnitude of charge on an electron, e = 1.60218 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Any physically existing charge Q is an integral multiple of e: Q = ne, where n is a positive or negative integer. Fractional basic charge does not exist in nature.
  3. Charge is conserved: the algebraic sum of electric charges in any closed system remains constant — no exceptions have ever been discovered. (E.g. when a neutron decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, the net charge before and after is still zero.)
  4. Charge is a scalar and hence charges obey ordinary scalar addition (with sign).
  5. Charge is relativistically invariant — its value doesn't change with relative motion of the observer.
✏️ Solved Example
Q. In Millikan's oil drop experiment, the charges on four different drops were found to be 1 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, 4 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, 5 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, and 7 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C. What do you infer from these results?
  1. Each measured charge is a whole-number multiple of 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C.
  2. This is exactly the elementary charge e — the charge on a single electron/proton.
🎯 Answer: Charge is quantised: every observed charge is an integral multiple (n = 1, 4, 5, 7) of the elementary charge e.

3 Electrification — How Bodies Get Charged

Electrification is the process of charging an uncharged body. In their normal state, bodies are electrically neutral (equal numbers of protons and electrons). Rubbing transfers electrons from one body to the other.

🖊️ Bringing a charged rod near a neutral conductor redistributes its charge — induced charges appear at each end.
  1. Electrification by friction: rubbing a glass rod with silk transfers electrons from the glass to the silk — the glass becomes positively charged (deficit of electrons), the silk negatively charged (excess of electrons). Substances can be ranked in a series (Table 1.1 in the text) — whichever occurs earlier becomes positive when rubbed against one occurring later.
  2. Electrification by induction: bringing a charged rod near (without touching) an uncharged conductor causes electrons to redistribute — the near end gets an induced charge opposite to the rod's, the far end gets a charge of the same sign as the rod. This is temporary: removing the rod lets the charges redistribute and disappear. A conductor can be permanently charged by induction using two touching spheres, separating them while the charged rod is still nearby.
  3. Electrification by conduction: touching a charged conductor to an uncharged one transfers some charge of the same sign to the second conductor — the amount depends on the size/shape of each.
⚠️ Don't lose marks: Charging by induction never transfers charge of the same sign as the inducing rod to the near side — the near end always gets the opposite sign. Mixing this up is a very common error.

4 Point Charge & Coulomb's Law

A point charge is any charged body whose dimensions are negligible compared to its distance from the point where its effect is being studied — even a charged body as large as the Earth can be treated as a point charge if we're only interested in its effect as far away as the Sun.

🖊️ Coulomb's law: like charges repel with equal and opposite forces along the line joining them.

Coulomb's law states that the force of attraction or repulsion between two point charges at rest is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them.

📐 Coulomb's law
F = K0 · Q1Q2 / r² (K0 = constant of proportionality, depends on system of units and medium)
In free space (SI): F = (1/4πε0) · Q1Q2 / r²

5 Coulomb's Law and Units of Charge

  • CGS-esu system: K0 is set equal to unity, defining the statcoulomb — the charge which, placed 1 cm from an identical charge in vacuum, repels it with a force of 1 dyne.
  • SI system: the measured value of K0 is 8.98755 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² (≈ 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻²). One coulomb is defined as the charge which, placed 1 m from an identical charge in vacuum, repels it with a force of 9 × 10⁹ N.
📐 Permittivity of free space
K0 = 1/(4πε0)
ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻² (permittivity of free space)
✏️ Solved Example
Q. Find the relation between coulomb and statcoulomb.
  1. Consider two point charges of q coulomb each, r metres apart, in free space.
  2. SI Coulomb force: F = 9×10⁹ q²/r² Newton
  3. Same setup in c.g.s-esu (converting q coulomb to x·q statcoulomb, r metres to 100r cm): F = (xq)²/(100r)² dyne = 10⁻⁵ N
  4. Equating the two expressions for F and solving gives x² = 9×10¹⁸, so x = 3×10⁹
🎯 Answer: 1 coulomb = 3 × 10⁹ statcoulomb

6 Effect of Medium — Permittivity & Dielectric Constant

Coulomb's law as written above (F = Q1Q2/4πε0r²) holds for charges in vacuum. If a material medium fills the space between the charges, the force is reduced. This is accounted for by replacing ε0 with ε, the permittivity of the medium.

📐 Force in a medium
F = Q1Q2 / (4πε r²) where ε = ε0 · εr (εr = relative permittivity = dielectric constant K)
F(medium) = F(vacuum) / K

Dielectric constant (K) is dimensionless, with the same value in cgs and SI systems. Its minimum value is 1 (vacuum/air) and its maximum is infinite (for good conductors).

Typical dielectric constantsAir ≈ 1.0006 · Water 80–83 · Mica 5.6–6.6 · Glass 6–10 · Oil 2–2.2

7 Principle of Superposition & Force Due to Multiple Charges

Coulomb's law by itself only tells us the force between two point charges. The principle of superposition extends this: the force between any two charges in a group is independent of the presence of all other charges — the net force on any one charge is simply the vector sum of the individual Coulomb forces due to every other charge.

🖊️ The resultant force on a charge is the vector sum of the individual Coulomb forces from every other charge.
🧮 Derivation: Net force on a charge due to n other charges
Given / Setup: Charges Q1, Q2, ... Qn at position vectors r1, r2, ... rn from a charge Q at the origin.
  1. Force on Q due to Q1 alone: F1 = (1/4πε0)(QQ1/r1²) r̂1, and similarly for F2, F3, ... Fn.
  2. By the superposition principle, these individual forces add vectorially (they don't interfere with each other).
✅ F = F1 + F2 + ... + Fn = (Q/4πε0) Σ (Qi/ri²) r̂i
✏️ Solved Example
Q. Three identical charges, each of charge q, are placed at the three vertices of an equilateral triangle of side a. Find the force on any one charge due to the other two.
  1. Force from each neighbouring charge has the same magnitude: F1 = F2 = q²/(4πε0a²).
  2. The angle between F1 and F2 is 60° (from the triangle's geometry), so the resultant bisects this angle.
  3. Resultant magnitude: F = √(F1² + F2² + 2F1F2cos60°) = √3 · q²/(4πε0a²).
🎯 Answer: F = √3 q² / (4πε0 a²), directed along the bisector, away from the triangle.
✏️ Solved Example
Q. Two identical pith balls, each of mass 19.6×10⁻⁵ kg, are suspended from the same point by two silk threads each 1.0 m long. They carry identical charge and repel each other so the threads make an angle of 90° at the point of suspension. Find the charge on each ball.
  1. By symmetry, each thread makes 45° with the vertical.
  2. For equilibrium: T cosθ = mg and T sinθ = F, so F = mg tanθ = mg (since θ = 45°, tan45° = 1).
  3. F also equals q²/(4πε0r²) where r is the separation between the balls.
  4. Solving q² = 4πε0 r² mg for q (with r worked out from the geometry) gives q ≈ 0.65 μC.
🎯 Answer: q ≈ 0.65 μC on each ball

8 Salient Features of Coulomb Interaction

  • Coulomb's law is an experimental law (established by Coulomb using a torsion balance).
  • It applies to point charges.
  • The Coulomb force between any two charges is unaffected by other charges nearby.
  • It is a central force (acts along the line joining the two charges).
  • It is a conservative force (work done is path-independent).
  • It is an unsaturated force — a single charge can simultaneously interact with any number of other charges.
  • It is a long-range force.
Coulomb force vs. gravitational forceBoth are central, long-range, conservative forces obeying an inverse-square law. But Coulomb force can be attractive or repulsive (gravity is only attractive), depends on the intervening medium (gravity doesn't), and is about 10³⁶ times stronger than the gravitational force between the same pair of particles.

9 Continuous Charge Distribution

Charge is fundamentally discrete (always an integral multiple of e), so strictly speaking a 'continuous' charge distribution isn't microscopically real. But for macroscopic bodies with enormous numbers of elementary charges, it's extremely useful to treat charge as continuously spread — exactly like treating a fluid as continuous in hydrodynamics despite it being made of molecules.

🖊️ Three ways to describe continuously distributed charge, depending on the shape of the charged body.
📐 Charge density definitions
Linear charge density: λ = Δq/Δℓ (charge per unit length; used for wires/rods) — q = ∫λ dℓ
Surface charge density: σ = Δq/ΔS (charge per unit area; used for charged sheets/plates) — q = ∫σ dS
Volume charge density: ρ = Δq/ΔV (charge per unit volume; used for charged solids) — q = ∫ρ dV

⚡ Quick Recap

  • Charge is conserved, quantised (Q = ne), and a scalar that adds algebraically.
  • Electrification: by friction (permanent transfer), induction (temporary, opposite charge on the near side), or conduction (same-sign charge transferred).
  • Coulomb's law: F = Q1Q2/(4πε0r²) in vacuum, reduced by a factor K (dielectric constant) in a medium.
  • Superposition principle: net force on a charge = vector sum of individual Coulomb forces from every other charge.
  • Coulomb force is central, conservative, unsaturated, long-range, and ~10³⁶ times stronger than gravity between the same particles.
  • Continuous charge distributions use linear (λ), surface (σ), or volume (ρ) charge density depending on geometry.

🎯 Exam Corner — practice in CHSE question style

These questions are adapted directly from the Model Questions at the end of this chapter in Bureau's Higher Secondary Physics — several are themselves drawn from past CHSE/CBSE papers, as cited in the textbook.

1 mark
  1. State Coulomb's law of electric force between two charged bodies. [CHSE 1989]
  2. What is the value and dimensional formula for ε0?
  3. Is Coulomb's force between two electrons greater than the gravitational force between them? If so, by what factor?
2 marks
  1. What do you understand by the principle of superposition of Coulomb forces?
  2. Give two points of distinction between electric charge and mass.
  3. Two charges +1μC and +5μC are placed 0.1 cm apart. What is the ratio of the Coulomb force acting on each of the two charges? [CHSE 2002]
3 marks
  1. Two point charges 4Q and Q are situated a distance d apart. Find the position of a third charge q so that it is in equilibrium, and discuss whether this equilibrium is stable or unstable.
  2. The distance between the electron and proton in a hydrogen atom is 0.53 Å. Find the electric force of attraction between them.
  3. Three equal charges, each of 1 μC, are placed at the three corners of an equilateral triangle of side 1 m. Find the force experienced by each charge due to the other two.
5 marks
  1. Discuss the different characteristics of electric charge. What do you mean by conservation of charge?
  2. What do you mean by electrification? Discuss the various ways of electrifying a body. How can a conductor be charged positively by induction?
  3. State Coulomb's law in electrostatics and hence define the unit of charge in the e.s.u and SI systems.
  4. State the principle of superposition in electrostatics. Obtain an expression for the force on one charge due to all other charges present in its neighbourhood.
