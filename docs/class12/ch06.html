Electromagnetic Induction

Move a magnet near a wire and — with no battery at all — current appears.

1 Faraday's Laws & Lenz's Law

🖊️ Moving a magnet near a coil changes flux through it, inducing an EMF and current.
📐 Faraday's law of EM induction
ε = −dΦ/dt (Φ = magnetic flux = B·A cosθ)

The negative sign (Lenz's law) says the induced current always opposes the very change that produced it — this is really just conservation of energy in disguise: you must do work to push the magnet in precisely because the induced current fights back.

🧮 Derivation: Motional EMF
Given / Setup: A rod of length l moves with velocity v perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field B, sliding on rails.
  1. Free electrons in the rod experience a magnetic force F = e(v×B), pushing them to one end.
  2. This charge separation creates an electric field inside the rod; equilibrium is reached when electric force balances magnetic force: eE = evB → E = vB
  3. EMF = E × l (potential difference across the rod)
✅ ε = Bvl
✏️ Solved Example
Q. A rod of length 0.5 m moves at 4 m/s perpendicular to a magnetic field of 0.2 T. Find the induced EMF.
  1. ε = Bvl = 0.2 × 4 × 0.5
🎯 Answer: ε = 0.4 V

2 Self and Mutual Induction

  • Self-inductance (L): a coil induces an EMF in itself when its own current changes — opposes any sudden change in current (like electrical inertia).
  • Mutual inductance (M): a changing current in one coil induces EMF in a nearby coil — the basic principle behind transformers.
📐 Induced EMF from inductance
ε = −L(dI/dt) (self)
ε2 = −M(dI1/dt) (mutual)
⚠️ Don't lose marks: Lenz's law direction questions are commonly answered backward under pressure — always re-derive the direction using 'induced current opposes the change in flux', don't just guess.

⚡ Quick Recap

  • Faraday's law: induced EMF = −rate of change of magnetic flux.
  • Lenz's law: induced current opposes the change producing it (energy conservation).
  • Self-inductance opposes change in its own current; mutual inductance links two coils.
